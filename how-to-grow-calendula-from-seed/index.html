About Calendula Officinalis

Calendula (Calendula officinalis L.), also known as pot marigold, is a flowering plant in the daisy family (Asteraceae). It is crucial not to confuse it with true marigolds (Tagetes), as they do not possess the same herbal properties.

According to Wisconsin Horticulture Division of Extension, Calendula officinalis is the specific species grown for its edible petals and herbal uses.

Grow calendula plants as hardy annuals in zones 2-10. While they readily self-seed in favorable conditions, in colder regions they are typically grown as single-season flowers and replanted each spring.

Calendula plants grow in a compact, bushy form, reaching 18-24 inches in height and 12 inches in width. This makes them perfect for:

  • Edging pathways: Their manageable size and vibrant blooms make calendula a versatile addition to any garden!
  • Garden beds
  • Containers

Calendula flowers are daisy-like with rows of overlapping petals in vibrant orange to yellow hues. Each 2–3-inch bloom features dense layers of petals around a central disk, and these cheerful flowers are highly attractive to pollinators, especially bees and butterflies.

Calendula flowers are edible with mild, peppery petals that can be used fresh in salads or dried for teas. The vibrant petals are often used to add natural color to dishes and have traditionally been used as a saffron substitute in cooking.

Calendula offers dual benefits in the garden - their abundant blooms provide cheerful color, and when planted alongside vegetables, they help deter common garden pests like tomato hornworms and asparagus beetles.

Choosing the Best Calendula Variety for Medicine

While all Calendula officinalis are edible and herbal, some have been bred specifically for high resin content (the sticky stuff that heals!). If your goal is to make potent salves, choosing the right variety matters.

1. The herbal Powerhouse: Resina (or Alpha)

If you are growing for a medicine cabinet, this is the gold standard.

  • Why Grow It: These blooms have the highest content of resins (sticky, antimicrobial compounds). When you touch the flower head, your fingers should feel tacky—that is the medicine!
  • Appearance: Often has smaller, simpler yellow-orange flowers compared to showy ornamentals, but packs a much stronger punch for skin soothing.

2. The Balanced Choice: Pacific Beauty

A wonderful dual-purpose variety that balances beauty with utility.

  • Why Grow It: It produces large, double blooms in shades of apricot, cream, and orange. It has moderate resin content, making it great for beginner herbalists who also want a stunning cut-flower garden.

3. The Resin-Rich Heirloom: Erfurter Orangefarbige

An older commercial strain cultivated specifically for pharmaceutical use.

  • Why Grow It: Known for its deep orange petals and exceptional yield of herbal compounds.

4. The "Showy" Varieties (Pink Surprise, Touch of Red)

These are bred for unique colors (pinks and reds) rather than potency.

  • Why Grow It: Perfect for edible flower garnishes in salads or simply enjoying their beauty. They can still be used in tea, but they are less potent for infused oils.

Grower's Tip: For the most potent soothing oil, look for varieties with deep orange petals, as they typically contain higher levels of carotenoids than the yellow or cream varieties.

What Do Calendula Seeds Look Like

Calendula flower seed are quite the curiosity. They are curved, rough-textured, and about 1/2 inch long. They have a distinctive C-shape, making them easy to identify when you're ready to start planting or harvest calendula seeds from your mature plants.

How to Grow Calendula from seed

Calendula is beginner-friendly and easy to grow. You can sow the seeds directly outdoors in your garden once the soil is workable, or start them indoors in a seed tray under a domed cover for controlled moisture and faster germination. This flexibility makes it simple to grow healthy seedlings ready for transplanting.

Seeds can be obtained from mature seed pods shared by other gardeners or purchased from reputable sources.

Calendula readily self-seeds, often producing volunteer plants that pop up in unexpected places throughout your garden the following spring. These volunteers can be easily transplanted while young, or you can leave them to create beautiful natural drifts of color.

While technically an annual, calendula can behave like a short-lived perennial in mild climates with proper deadheading. In zones 8-10, plants may bloom continuously nearly year-round, pausing only during the hottest months of summer.

Calendula grows quickly, taking just 45-60 days from seed to first bloom. Once established, they'll flower continuously until frost, providing months of colorful blooms and herbal flowers. It is a rewarding plant for any garden!

Calendula plants prefer well-draining soil but are adaptable to most conditions. Regular watering and deadheading spent blooms will keep them flowering abundantly.

In mild winters, calendula continues blooming. In cold regions, they die back after frost but will often self-seed for next year's garden.

Calendula Seed Saving

Wait until the flower heads are completely dry and brown, then collect the curved seeds from the center of the flower.

Harvesting Calendula Flowers

To harvest calendula flowers, pick the freshly opened blooms on a sunny morning when they're dry, snipping the stem just below the calyx leaving a bit of stem attached.

After harvesting calendula blooms, they will continue producing. The more you pick, the more they grow.

A healthy calendula plant will product dozens of blooms throughout the growing season, with a single plant producing 100-200 flowers over several months if regularly harvested.

While most flowers are harvested at peak bloom, it's wonderful to let some go to seed - not only for their continued beauty in the garden, but also because they will self-seed readily, ensuring next season's plants.

The pollinators, especially bees and butterflies, will thank you too!
